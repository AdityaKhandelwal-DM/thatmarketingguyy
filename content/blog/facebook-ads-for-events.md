An event has a date, and Facebook ads for events only make sense if you plan around it. Ads that run all year with no deadline get scrolled past. Ads that count down to a date, then stop, give people a reason to act now. This guide covers both kinds of business I see: someone selling tickets to a one-off event, and a venue that sells dates for weddings, parties and corporate bookings.

Some of what follows comes from my own campaigns for venues in India. Those costs are Indian costs, so I'll show a dollar figure next to the rupees, and you should expect to pay more per lead in the US, UK, UAE, Australia or Singapore.

## Do Facebook ads work for events?

Yes, when the ad has one clear action and a deadline: buy a ticket, book a date or start a chat. They work badly when you boost a post and hope. Meta runs Facebook and Instagram ads together, so one campaign can reach people on both, and event audiences are usually on both.

## What's different about events and venues

Three things change how I build the campaign compared with an ordinary local business.

- **Every event has a deadline.** Budget ramps up as the date gets close, and the ads stop the day it is over. Evergreen "always on" campaigns suit venues, not one-off events.
- **There are two different products.** A ticketed event sells seats before a date. A venue sells dates: wedding enquiries, birthday parties, corporate bookings. They need different campaign objectives, and mixing them in one account is a common mistake.
- **Past attendees are your best audience.** People who came last time, enquired before or follow you already know the venue. Custom audiences built from them usually beat interest targeting. Meta explains how in its [Business Help Centre](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/744354708981227).

## How to set up Facebook ads for an event, step by step

### 1. Pick the right objective

For tickets, send people to the page where they can buy, and tell Meta to optimise for purchases. For a venue, the sale happens in a conversation, so ask for enquiries: an instant lead form or a WhatsApp or Messenger chat. Don't optimise a venue for clicks. Clicks are cheap and rarely turn into a booked date.

### 2. Build the audience in layers

Start with people who already know you: past attendees, enquirers, followers and your email list. Then add a lookalike of those people, and a tight local radius around the venue. Use broad interests last, if at all. A local event rarely needs more than that.

### 3. Plan the countdown

Here is the shape I use for a ticketed event. The weeks are an example, not a rule, so change them to suit your date and ticket price.

1. **Announcement (weeks 6 to 4 before):** small budget, warm audiences, a clear date and price.
2. **Build-up (weeks 4 to 2):** add the lookalike and local audiences, new creative each week.
3. **Last push (final 10 days):** the largest budget, "limited tickets" and "this Saturday" messages.
4. **Retargeting (final 3 days):** only people who visited the ticket page and didn't buy.

Turn everything off the moment the event is sold out or over.

### 4. Write the offer and the creative

Show the real place and the real crowd. A short phone video of the venue, a clip from the last event, or a table laid out for a wedding does more than a designed poster. Put the date, the price or "from" price and one button in the first line of text. If a creative stops working, swap it quickly. In my farmhouse venue campaign below, I replaced the creative the moment it fatigued, and that is what kept one campaign scalable.

### 5. Choose a form, a chat or a ticket page

- **Ticket page:** best when the buyer can pay in a minute. It must load fast on a phone.
- **Instant lead form:** good for weddings and corporate enquiries. Ask only for name, phone, date and guest count.
- **WhatsApp or Messenger chat:** good for parties and private events, where people want to ask about price and availability first.

### 6. Track the real result

Count tickets sold, enquiries and chats. For a venue, keep a simple sheet that records which enquiries became booked dates. Without it you can't tell a cheap lead from a good one.

### 7. Set the budget

Work back from the price and the capacity. As a hypothetical example, if a ticket costs $40, you need 200 sales and expect 1 in 20 people who reach the ticket page to buy, you can calculate what each page visit is worth before you spend. If the maths doesn't work, change the price or the offer first. Raising the budget won't fix it.

## Ad examples you can adapt

These are examples to adapt, not real client ads.

- **Ticketed event:** "Saturday 14 March. Live music, food stalls, 300 tickets only. Get yours before they're gone." Button: Buy tickets.
- **Wedding venue:** "Looking at wedding dates for next season? See the lawns, the hall and the rooms, then tell us your date." Button: Get quote.
- **Party venue:** "Birthday parties for 20 or more, one price per guest, a room for the cake. Message us the date." Button: Send message.

## What results to expect

Judge the campaign by cost per ticket, per enquiry or per chat, not by reach. These are my own venue campaigns in India, shown to illustrate each model. They are not what you will pay abroad.

- **Wedding resort, lead ads:** always-on wedding lead ads plus a daycation package for the off-season. 562 leads at ₹138 (about $1.65) each, 1.8L (180,000) reach, on ₹78K (about $935) of spend.
- **Farmhouse event venue, chats:** one scaled WhatsApp campaign, creative refreshed as it tired. ₹6.36 (about $0.08) per chat, ₹1.46 per click, 8.9L (890,000) reach, ₹32K (about $385) spend.
- **Family entertainment centre, chats:** always-on WhatsApp conversation ads at city scale. ₹18 (about $0.22) per chat, 37.8L (3.78 million) reach, ₹2.6L (about $3,100) spend.

A chat or a lead is not a booked date or a sold ticket. Cheap enquiries still need someone to reply quickly, and the best campaign cannot fix a slow reply. For a sense of what ad budgets usually buy, see my post on [how much Facebook ads cost](/blog/facebook-ads-cost).

## Mistakes I see most

- Running the same ad for weeks with no countdown or change
- Optimising for clicks instead of tickets or enquiries
- Mixing ticket sales and venue enquiries in one campaign
- Ignoring past attendees and enquirers as an audience
- Leaving enquiries unanswered for a day
- Leaving ads on after the event, or switching them off too early

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance should I start Facebook ads for an event?

For a ticketed event, usually four to six weeks, with the biggest budget in the last ten days. That is a typical starting point, not a rule. A large festival may need longer and a small class much less.

### Should I send event ads to a ticket page or a lead form?

If people can buy a ticket online, send them to the ticket page. If your sale happens in a conversation, such as a wedding, a party or a corporate booking, use a lead form or a chat.

### How much should I spend to promote an event on Facebook?

Work back from ticket price and capacity. As a hypothetical example, if you want 200 sales at $40 and can afford to spend 10% of revenue on ads, your ad budget is $800. Start lower, test, and scale only what sells.
