The clearest example is a skin clinic in Jaipur. It started with an empty appointment book and low walk-ins. Here is the setup, and the parts that matter if you run a clinic in the US, UK, UAE, Australia or Singapore.

## Can clinics advertise on Facebook?

Yes, clinics can advertise on Facebook and Instagram, but health is a sensitive category and Meta's rules are stricter than for, say, a café. Ads can't make claims that imply you know something personal about the viewer, such as a medical condition, and exaggerated before-and-after promises are a fast route to a rejected ad.

I keep clinic ads about the service, the doctor and the booking, not the problem on the viewer's face or body. Meta's policies change, so check the current health and wellness rules for your country before you spend. If an ad is rejected, I'd rather fix the wording than argue.

## The setup behind the 78%

I can only give you what's on the record: Meta lead ads for the enquiry, and the Google map listing worked at the same time so that people who searched for the clinic found it ready to book. The ads started the interest. The listing caught the people who then looked the clinic up.

The principles I'd apply to any clinic:

1. **One treatment per campaign.** Not "our clinic", but one service with one clear offer, such as a consultation.
2. **A real person in the creative.** The doctor or the team, not a stock photo.
3. **A lead form, not a website visit.** Fewer steps means more enquiries.
4. **A fast callback.** The leads are only worth something if someone answers.
5. **A listing that backs up the ad.** Hours, reviews and a booking button, all up to date.

## What should a clinic's lead form ask?

A clinic's lead form should ask for the minimum: name, phone number, and the treatment they're interested in. Every extra field loses people, and a clinic needs a real conversation to book anyway.

I'd add one question that filters, such as the treatment or preferred day, and nothing about medical history. Collecting health details in an ad form is a privacy risk you don't need, and it's better handled on the phone. Check your local rules on health data, as they differ between countries.

Then set a rule in the clinic: someone calls within an hour in opening hours. This is where most clinic campaigns quietly fail. In my own results, a nightlife venue was paying ₹2,264 (about $27) per lead because the form leads were never being called back. I restructured it to a conversation objective. The ad wasn't the problem, the follow-up was.

## Does it get cheaper with better targeting of intent?

A hair transplant clinic I worked with is a good example. High-ticket consultations were buried under unqualified enquiries. I moved to conversation ads with creative tuned for intent instead of volume. Cost per chat went from ₹93 to ₹33 (about $1.11 to $0.40), on ₹92K of spend (about $1,100), with 12.5L reach.

The lesson: a cheaper enquiry is only good if it's from someone who could realistically become a patient. I'd always rather have fewer, better enquiries.
